Visual direction

Rider, water, horizon

The selected reference establishes three useful layers: handlebars close to the viewer, a broad field of water, and a distant horizon. Preserve that relationship. It gives the visitor a body position, a surface to read, and an orientation anchor. The published photograph is a framing reference, not a texture source or a promise to reproduce its location.

The proposed craft should be an original clean sport design. A compact center display and visible grips can carry the essential controls. Avoid excessive branding, ornamental gauges, giant steering wheels, and a hood that hides the next wave. The rider's hands may be visible, but their animation should communicate steering and contact rather than distract with constant gestures.

Water language

Water needs readable large forms before micro-detail. Broad swells establish a route, medium waves suggest approach timing, and small surface highlights provide speed. Foam should gather around contact and disturbed water rather than cover the entire surface. Reflection and color should make the water feel deep without turning it into mirror-polished metal.

Use clear daylight with a single reliable sun direction for the first concept. Turquoise and blue-green water, a pale sky, neutral craft materials, and a limited course-marker accent are sufficient. Keep bloom restrained. The horizon should remain visible through spray, highlights, and any landing effect.

Course and camera

The coast is a distant compositional anchor, not an invitation to build a large explorable city. One landmark, a few shoreline masses, buoys, and ramps can support a compact authored course. Gate colors and silhouettes should distinguish route information from decoration. Ramps should look usable from the approach angle before any prompt appears.

A low rider camera makes speed believable. Preserve enough forward clearance to see the landing zone. Default stabilization should reduce involuntary roll while retaining pitch and hull response. Do not use dramatic camera cuts to conceal an unresolved landing.

Interface and art requirements

The speed readout lives on the craft. Gate progress and time sit at the top edge. Contextual hints use small corner or grip-adjacent labels, then disappear. A central landing word can appear briefly after impact, but long score panels belong in results.

Desktop and landscape phone compositions need separate control studies. The touch layout must not place a thumb over the next gate. The first visual approval should include cruising, approaching a crest, airborne, landing, pause, and recovery compositions. Those remain requested future art states; only the existing photographic reference is delivered in this documentation pack.
